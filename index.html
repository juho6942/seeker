Seeker: a pricing and dispatch model for car transport

A car-transport dispatcher for one simulated week in Finland. It prices about 1,000 dealer requests against a hidden competitor, then pays freelance drivers to move every car it wins. Everything it does follows from one estimate: the fee at which a driver takes this car in time.

Overview

We are a middleman. A dealer pays us a price to move a car, and we pay a freelance driver a fee to do it. The score is what is left over, and it only counts if at least 90% of the cars arrive on time.

40krevenue from dealers− 24kdriver fees− 0.8klate refunds≈ 15kweekly margin

Rough sizes from early practice weeks. Seeker's weeks average 18.7k. Driver fees are the largest lever: 0.1 × the typical fee across all gigs is about 2.5k a week.

The difficulty is that almost everything that decides the outcome is hidden. The model is easiest to understand as five estimates of hidden things, each feeding one decision.

One request, start to finish

  1. A dealer asks for a price. The request names the lane, the car, up to three deadline options and sometimes a free-text note.
  2. We work out what a driver would cost. For each option the planner finds the cheapest fee schedule and the chance that someone books in time. Options that are too risky are dropped, risky ones are sold dearer.
  3. We pick the price with the best expected profit. A higher price earns more per car but wins less often. The balance point depends on where the competitor sits this week, which the model has been learning from every earlier answer.
  4. The dealer answers. Won or lost is the only feedback we get, and it updates the competitor estimate either way.
  5. A won car goes to the driver board. If another open car of ours makes a sensible chain, both are offered to one driver. Otherwise it is posted alone, starting at a low fee that rises on a schedule.
  6. The fee climbs until a driver books. Slowly at first, faster as the last possible pickup time approaches, and back down once the car is late anyway.
  7. The car is delivered. On time builds the dealer's trust in us, which raises the price that dealer accepts next time. Late halves it and costs a refund.

System diagram

Two loops share one set of offline-fitted tables. The top loop sells: a request comes in, quote() answers, the dealer's choice teaches learn() the competitor's price this week. The bottom loop delivers: a won car is posted to the driver board, alone or paired, with a fee schedule from fees().

The highlighted boxes are the four functions in algo.ts (416 lines, every decision), with their steps listed inside. Dashed lines are table lookups, and each table names the functions that read it. The engine (core/engine.ts) does the mechanics and calls the four. The numbered sections below give the formulas behind each box.

Main ideas

The design choices that matter most. Each is covered in detail in the numbered sections below.

One booking model behind every decision

The model estimates the fee at which a driver takes a car in time. Which options we sell, the price we quote, the fee schedule and the pairing of cars all read it from the same booking model, so the parts cannot disagree.

Competitor price learned from wins and losses

The competitor's price is never shown; we only learn whether we won. A small filter turns every answer into an estimate of this week's price level, per weekday and per lane, and the next quote already uses it.

worth +419 ± 101 and +303 ± 83 EUR a week

Competitor outage estimated as a probability

Once a week the competitor goes down in one region. The model holds a probability over 31 possibilities (none, or around each of 30 towns) and prices against a blend of the competitor and its dearer backup.

worth +430 ± 144 EUR a week

Realized cost in the price choice

The planned driver fee explained 6% of what an order ended up costing. Trees fitted on 156,000 finished orders explain 27%, and using them in the price choice was one of the largest gains.

worth +644 ± 106 EUR a week

Driver capacity priced into every quote

About 200 drivers carry everything, so driver time has a price inside every quote: 4 EUR per driver-hour. A full town or a full deadline day costs the dealer 25% more instead of getting a no.

worth +600 to +800 EUR a week

Limits on the fee schedule

The booking model shows that paying above 1.4 × the typical fee buys no extra bookings, so the ramp stops at 1.45. Once a car is late whatever we pay, the fee drops back to 0.83 instead of climbing.

fee floor and late fee: +530 ± 116 EUR a week

Testing method

Each rule was kept only if it earned margin on fresh server weeks with the same seeds, usually 48 to 96 of them. Ideas that did not pass were removed. On 96 fresh practice weeks the final model averages 18,738 EUR a week, with 95 of 96 weeks ranked.

Code structure

Every decision lives in one 416-line file with four functions, and every tunable value in one table. The engine can be restarted in the middle of the final and resumes from the server's state and its own saved learning.

1. The booking model and the fee planner

A logistic model answers one question: what is the chance that a driver books this gig within the next hour, at this fee? It was fitted on 160 weeks played with random fees. It comes first because both the prices (section 2) and the driver fees (section 4) are built on it.

\[ p_{\text{book}}(t, r) \;=\; \sigma\Big( -9.33 \;+\; \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_j \beta_j\,x_j(t)}_{\text{gig and clock}} \;+\; \underbrace{5.20\,r \;-\; 3.25\,\max(0,\,r-1) \;-\; 2.91\,\max(0,\,r-1.4)}_{\text{fee } r \text{ as a multiple of the typical fee}} \Big) \]
Fee term of the logit, by fee multiple r

Slope 5.2 up to the typical fee, 1.95 from 1.0 to 1.4, then −0.97: in the model, paying above 1.4 × typical buys no extra bookings. This is why the early ramp stops at 1.45.

Other weights (logit units)

Reading the bars: a gig is easier to book in the morning, as a two-car gig, and when its typical fee is large. It is harder at a private pickup address, late in the evening, on Sunday, for premium cars (experienced drivers only), with long drives, and the longer it has already sat on the board.

The fee planner

For a gig posted now with \(H\) hours to its last pickup time (at most 72), the planner tries every opening fee from 0.706 to 1.6 in steps of 0.05 and every hourly raise in {0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2}, and walks the hours:

\[ r_k = \text{open} + \text{raise}\cdot\min(k,\,4), \qquad p_k = \sigma\big(z_k + b + f(r_k)\big), \qquad U_0 = 1,\;\; U_{k+1} = U_k\,(1-p_k) \]
\[ \text{expected cost} \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^{H-1} U_k\,p_k\,r_k \;+\; U_H \times 4 \qquad\quad \text{chance booked in time} \;=\; 1-U_H \]

\(z_k\) is the gig-and-clock part of the logit in hour \(k\), \(f\) the fee term above, and 4 × typical is what a gig without a driver is counted as (lateCost). The cheapest (open, raise) pair wins. Raises stop after 4 steps.

The booking shift. \(b\) = bookShift = 0.2 is added to the logit, but only when planning fee schedules. In quote() the planner runs with \(b = 0\). The booking model is a little too pessimistic about how fast drivers book, so the schedules can open lower, while which options are sold and at what price stays the same. Alone this was worth about +300 a week with orders unchanged.

2. Prices: quote()

Each request offers up to three delivery options (same day, next day, within 3 days). The model decides which options to sell, picks one markup for the whole request, then adjusts each option's price.

Step 1. Which options are sellable

For every option the fee planner (section 1) returns the cheapest schedule, its expected cost, and the chance a driver books before the last pickup time. The last pickup time is the deadline minus the drive, moved by anything the dealer's note says (car ready later, deliver earlier, real site hours).

  • Does the last pickup time leave at least 3 h after the car is ready?
    • nodrop the option
    • yesIs the last pickup time still ahead of now?
      • nodrop the option
      • yesChance a driver books in time (planner)?
        • < 0.82drop the option
        • 0.82 to 0.95sell as a hard option, price × 1.20
        • ≥ 0.95sell at the normal price
  • No option left? Decline. The engine then sends a quote at 10 × list instead of staying silent: losing is free, silence costs goodwill.

Step 2. What the car will cost

The planner's expected fee is a weak predictor of what an order really ends up costing (held-out R² 0.06). Gradient-boosted trees fitted on 156,312 won orders predict the realized cost from 38 quote-time inputs (R² 0.27): fees with raises, refunds, failed visits, undelivered cars. Seeker over-weights the trees.

\[ \text{cost}_o \;=\; (1-w)\cdot \text{planFee}_o \;+\; w\cdot \widehat{c}_{\text{trees}}(x_o)\cdot \text{typical} \qquad w = \texttt{costW} = 1.25 \]

With w = 1.25 this is 1.25 × trees − 0.25 × plan: it pushes past the trees, away from the plan. typical is the lane-table typical fee, not the dealer's noisy number.

Step 3. The chance to win at a price

The competitor's price is never shown. The model keeps a running estimate of its log price for this request, \(\mu\), as a fixed part from the offline table plus this week's learned levels:

\[ \mu \;=\; \underbrace{c + \beta_1\ln(1+0.01\,\text{trust}) + \beta_2\tfrac{\text{drive}}{600} + \text{lane}_{ab} + \text{pick}_a + \text{del}_b + \text{hour}_h + \text{dealer}_d - 0.0098\,\text{lates}}_{g\ \text{(price.json; } c = 0.327 \Rightarrow 1.39\times\text{list on average)}} \;+\; \underbrace{x_{\text{week}} + x_{\text{weekday}} + x_{\text{lane}}}_{\text{learned this week}} \]
\[ P(\text{win}\mid m) \;=\; \sigma\!\left(\frac{\mu - \ln m}{s}\right), \qquad s=\sqrt{\,0.1^2 + 0.37\,v\,}, \qquad \sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}} \]

m is our price as a multiple of list. v is the filter's own variance for this request, so early in the week (large doubt) the curve is flatter. With s = 0.1, a 1% higher price costs about 10% of the win odds.

Between 08:00 and 18:00 the model also carries a belief that the competitor is down around this pickup today (share \(q\)), where dealers buy from a backup about 1.2 × dearer. The win chance becomes a mixture:

\[ P(\text{win}\mid m) \;=\; (1-q)\,\sigma\!\left(\frac{\mu-\ln m}{s}\right) \;+\; q\,\sigma\!\left(\frac{\mu+\ln 1.2-\ln m}{s}\right) \]

Step 4. One markup for the request

\[ m^{*} \;=\; \underset{m\,\in\,[\,1.28+\Delta,\;2.00+\Delta\,],\ \text{step } 0.01}{\arg\max}\;\; P(\text{win}\mid m)\,\Big(m\cdot\overline{\text{list}} \;-\; \overline{\text{cost}} \;-\; \underbrace{4\,\text{EUR}\times(\text{drive hours}+1)}_{\text{capacity cost}}\Big) \]

Bars mean the average over the sellable options. The capacity cost is a shadow price on driver time: drivers are the bottleneck, so a car that holds a driver longer must earn more. It is subtracted in the choice only, never paid.

The search window slides by \(\Delta\), the sum of these shifts (all in multiples of list):

ConditionShift ΔWhy
Pickup town full: our undelivered orders from it ≥ 3 × drivers living within 30 km+0.25Ration drivers by price instead of declining
Round trip: an open one-car gig of ours delivers within 40 km of this pickup and starts within 40 km of this delivery−0.05The driver needs no trip home
Outage belief at this pickup (share q)+0.30 × qThe dealer's other choice is the dearer backup
Cheap week or cheap day: learned level x_week + x_weekday below 0level, at most −0.20A fixed floor would lose whole days in a price war

Step 5. The price of each option

\[ \text{price}_o \;=\; \text{list}_o \times \big(m^{*} + d_o\big) \times \begin{cases}1.25 & \text{deadline day full}\\ 1 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases} \times \begin{cases}1.20 & \text{hard option}\\ 1 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases} \]
\[ d_o = \begin{cases} -0.03 & \text{due next Monday or later}\\ +0.08 & \text{due Friday}\\ 0 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases} \qquad\quad \text{quote option } o \iff \text{price}_o \ge 1.10\times\text{cost}_o \]

A deadline day (Mon to Fri) is full when our orders due that day reach 0.10 × the week's driver roster. Friday holds 40% of the lates, Monday deadlines have slack. If no option clears the 10% profit gate, the request is declined.

3. Learning during the week: learn()

Section 2 uses two quantities that change during the week: the competitor's price level and the chance of an outage. Both are updated after every answer.

The price filter

The competitor sits 26% to 54% above list, at a different level every week, with a weekday swing and a level per lane inside the week. The state \(x\) holds one number for the week, five for the weekdays and one per lane (pickup × delivery town, single cars, created when first asked). Every won or lost quote is one logistic observation; the update is a one-step Kalman-style correction.

\[ \hat p = \sigma\!\left(\frac{g + h^{\top}x - \ln m}{0.1}\right), \qquad w=\hat p\,(1-\hat p), \qquad a = \frac{P\,h}{0.1}, \qquad k = \frac{w}{1 + w\,h^{\top}a/0.1} \]
\[ P \;\leftarrow\; P - k\,a\,a^{\top}, \qquad\quad x \;\leftarrow\; x + \frac{P\,h}{0.1}\,\big(\text{won} - \hat p\big) \]

\(h\) marks which levels this request touches. \(P\) starts diagonal with prior SDs in log price: week 0.05, weekday 0.14, lane 0.06. Winning more than expected raises the levels, and the next quotes go out dearer. Dealer and town levels exist in the code but have prior 0 (tested, no gain).

The outage belief

Once a week the competitor is down in one region (a town and everything within 80 km) for a working day. The model holds a probability over 31 hypotheses: no outage today, or an outage around each of the 30 towns.

\[ \text{prior}(\text{outage today}) = \min\!\Big(0.6,\ \frac{1}{5-\text{day}}\Big)\ \text{if not seen yet this week, split over towns by}\ \ 1 + \#\{\text{our requests near town } c\} \]
\[ \text{post}(c) \;\propto\; \text{prior}(c)\prod_{\text{today's answers } i} \begin{cases} \ell_i(\mu_i + \ln 1.2) & \text{pickup}_i \text{ within 80 km of } c\\ \ell_i(\mu_i) & \text{otherwise}\end{cases} \qquad q(\text{place}) = \sum_{c\,:\,\text{place near } c}\text{post}(c) \]

\(\ell_i\) is the chance of the answer we actually got (won or lost) at the price we quoted, clipped to 0.08 to 0.92 so one answer cannot decide it. \(q\) feeds the mixture in step 3 and the +0.30 × q shift in step 4. The price filter reads each answer against the expected price there, so an outage does not drag the whole weekday level up. Once the belief passes 0.9 on a day, the outage counts as seen and its prior is 0 for the rest of the week.

4. Driver fees: fees()

When a gig is posted, the planner from section 1 sets its opening fee and hourly raises. Fixed rules take over as the last pickup time approaches.

The life of a fee schedule

Example: gig posted 30 h before its last pickup time, plan = open 0.81, raise 0.05

Fee as a multiple of the typical fee, while nobody has booked. The opening fee and raise in this example are illustrative; the other levels are the model's parameters. The warning schedule restarts at 1.30, which is under the ramp's top of 1.45.

  • Has a deadline warning come for this order? (from the world 3 h before the last pickup time, or from the engine's own timer 3 h before the last pickup the note allows)
    • noNormal schedule
      • Planned opening fee, then up to 4 hourly raises
      • If still open: hourly ramp to 1.45 × typical, starting 10 h before the last pickup time (or 1 h after the last raise) and ending 3 h before it
      • Posted Thursday or later: every fee × 0.95. Monday to Wednesday: × 1
    • yesHas the last pickup time passed?
      • noSteep schedule: 1.30, then +0.15 every 30 minutes, 4 times (to 1.90). A pair is split first.
      • yesLate anyway: back down to 0.832, then +0.2 every 6 hours (to 1.63). Paying more no longer buys an on-time delivery.

5. Two-car gigs: pair()

A driver who takes two cars in a chain pays one trip to the first pickup and one trip home, and the booking model gives two-car gigs a large bonus (+1.13 in the logit). When a one-car order is won, the engine checks every open, unbooked one-car gig of ours in both orders (new car first, new car second).

  • Can the new order be paired at all?
    • noif its note says no plates or manual gearbox, moves the pickup or deadline, or names real site hours. Post alone.
    • yesFor each candidate chain: total drive ≤ 12 h and no car past its last pickup time? (partner must not be urgent or previously split)
      • yesFirst delivery within 40 km of the second pickup?
        • yesAt least 6 h of slack before the tighter last pickup time?
          • yesCandidate. Score = −join km − 0.5 × km from the last delivery back to the first pickup. Best score wins.
  • If a pair is chosen: withdraw the partner's gig, post one two-car gig. Its schedule comes from the same planner with the opening fee capped at 0.9 × the sum of both typical fees, then ramps to at most 1.3.
  • Exit: unbooked after 3 h, dropped by its driver, or a deadline warning: the pair is split, each car gets its own gig and is never paired again.

Known limits

  • Small on-time margin. 6 of 96 test weeks were under 92% on time and the worst was 89.2%, just under the 90% line, so about one week in 96 is unranked.
  • No further gain from parameter tuning. A screen of 14 more parameter changes around these values (pairKm 50, pairHoldH 2, minProfit 0.15, costUp 0.3 and others) found no edge.
  • High-cost deliveries late in the week. On Thursday and Friday, 26 to 35% of our deliveries cost over 80% of their price. Driver scarcity is priced with one flat rate, not one that tightens as the week fills up.
  • No reaction to the flu day. It shows as 6 or more drops between 00:00 and 01:00 and roughly doubles the late share of orders due that day. The model does not use the signal.
